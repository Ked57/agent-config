In one week this month, two companies that check other people's code raised money at valuations totaling $2 billion. Neither of them writes software for you. They read it, they test it, they tell you what is wrong with it. And that is now one of the more fundable things you can do in this industry. For 20 years the industry optimized one thing, how fast a developer could produce code. That problem is now substantially solved and the money is telling you where the new problem is. Start with the two rounds because they landed on the same day and that is not a coincidence. Code Rabbit raised $143 million at a $1.5 billion valuation. It reviews pull requests. It says it handles 2 million code reviews a week across 17,000 customers. Their own announcement is titled "Code is abundant, judgment is scarce." Which is a hell of a thing to put on a funding post. And it happens to be the thesis of this entire video. The same day Blacksmith raised $45 million at a valuation approaching $550 million. They make software testing infrastructure faster. Their customer count went from about 800 to over 6,000. Their CEO put it in one line, "Writing code has gotten dramatically easier. Validating it hasn't." And these two are not alone. Counting only rounds with named amounts, I could find roughly $494 million going into code review, testing, and verification companies. I want to be straight about that chart. Nobody publishes an official size for this category. That is my own tally of announced rounds and most of those companies never disclosed a valuation at all. So take the individual rounds as fact and the total as an estimate. Now, the more interesting question. Is the bottleneck real or is this just where venture money happens to be pointing this quarter? Here is the problem with most of the evidence you will see quoted on this. Nearly every statistic about review being the bottleneck comes from a company that sells a fix for the bottleneck. That does not make them false. It does mean the load-bearing claims in this film come from three places with nothing to sell. Start with the strongest one. Economists at MIT and Wharton studied more than 100,000 GitHub developers matched against their actual AI usage telemetry. Published through the National Bureau of Economic Research in May. They measured three generations of tools. After complete raised commits 40%, interactive agents 140, autonomous agents 180%. Enormous gains. Now, watch what happens to that 180% as you follow it downstream towards something a user can actually install. 180% more commits becomes 50% more projects and 50% more projects becomes 30% more actual releases. That collapse is the whole video. The paper calls it the weak link hypothesis. The strong productivity gains from AI are attenuated by human bottlenecks in the production chain. They even checked it against four app marketplaces. Moderate increase in the number of new apps. No increase in total usage. More software got made. Not more software got used. The authors put a number on how substitutable AI and human effort are in that chain. 0.25, meaning they are strong complements. The machine going faster does not remove the person. Second non-commercial source. Google's DORA research, roughly 5,000 professionals. Their finding is short. AI adoption does continue to have a negative relationship with software delivery stability. More output, less stability, which is precisely the shape you would expect if the checking step became the constraint. Third. Veracode tested more than 100 models on security. 45% of code samples failed. Java was worst at 72%. Cross-site scripting went undefended in 86% of the relevant samples. And the line that kills the obvious rebuttal, security performance stayed flat regardless of model size. The next model up does not fix this. Veracode came back to it this year with the driest sentence in the whole research pile. Two years of revolutionary model releases have moved the security needle from approximately 55% to approximately 55%. If the model cannot check itself and getting bigger does not help, then the check has to live outside the model. That is a product category and now you know why it is funded. Now, I promised to be careful with numbers, so let me show you one that gets quoted constantly and is completely wrong. You may have seen the claim that commits on GitHub went up 14 times. It traces to a tweet from GitHub's chief operating officer and he did not say that. He said GitHub was on pace for 14 billion commits this year if growth remains linear and then wrote in brackets, "Spoiler, it won't." He disclaimed his own projection in the same sentence. 14 billion became 14 times somewhere in transit. The measured figure is 25.1% year-over-year. That is a good number. It is not a tenfold anything. I am spending time on this because a film about measurement problems should not have one and because the same thing happened to the most quoted study in this space. You have probably heard that developers using AI are 19% slower while believing they were 20% faster. That is real, it is Meteors and it is 16 developers, not a survey of thousands. And Meteor themselves have since said they believe developers are likely more sped up now in early 2026 than their early 2025 estimates. They stamped their own page, "These results are out of date." So, the strongest skeptical evidence against the productivity story was retracted by the people who produced it. That is what integrity looks like and it is also why this film does not lean on it. What developers themselves report is more stable. In Stack Overflow's survey, 46% distrust the accuracy of AI output. 33% trust it. And the top frustration named by 66% is the phrase that should be printed on the side of every one of these tools, almost right, but not quite. Almost right is the expensive kind of wrong. Obviously broken code gets caught in seconds. Possible code gets merged and then it is somebody's problem in 6 months. So, who gets paid for this? That is the part that matters for your career and the answer is sitting in the hiring data. Dice publishes the skills growing fastest in tech job postings year over year. The list is one long argument for everything in this video. Maintainability, observability, responsible AI, cloud-native architecture, tooling, all growing over 200% and every one of them is about keeping systems trustworthy rather than producing more of them. One honest caveat on that. Dice publishes the list, not a number per skill. Anyone quoting you a precise growth figure for observability specifically is making it up. Put the pieces together and the picture is consistent from three directions that do not talk to each other. Academic economists measure the gains collapsing between commit and release. Security researchers measure the defects staying flat across model generations. And venture investors who are paid to be early are funding the check rather than the writing. What that means for you is narrower and more useful than most career advice about AI. One, being fast at producing code is now the commodity half of the job. The NBER paper measured that gain arriving and then evaporating on its way to a release. Two, the skills that survive this are the ones on that Dice list, making systems observable, maintainable, and safe to change. Boring words that now carry a 200% growth rate. Three, if you want to know where a market is going, watch what gets funded rather than what gets announced. $2 billion of valuation went to the checking step this month. And a caution, because this film has been hard on other people's numbers. The skeptical case here is genuinely thin. I went looking for a credible voice arguing this framing is overblown and did not find a strong one. The honest objection is methodological. Most vendor studies have no control group. The one big retraction came from the skeptical side. That is where the argument actually stands today, and it may not hold. For 20 years, the scarce thing was somebody who could build it. The scarce thing now is somebody who can tell whether it should ship. Code is abundant, judgment is scarce. A company put that on a funding announcement, and the market gave them $1.5 billion for agreeing.
